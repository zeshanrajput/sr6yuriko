Symlink

Warning

This narrative contains spoilers for SRM 2081-06 and 2081-18.

“You downed a freaking attack helicopter?” Brynne’s voice drifted from the bedroom, scraping rough against her throat before sharpening into disbelief. “In view of everyone in downtown Seattle? You call that ‘just another Sunday’?”

In the kitchen, Reiko adjusted the water flow, letting warm suds swirl across a cast-iron skillet. The scent of fried bacon and toasted sourdough hung thick in the air, rich and heavy against the quiet hum of the apartment’s climate scrubbers. “It was a very loud Sunday,” Reiko called back, the harmonic resonance in her vocal array softening with genuine warmth.

“And what’s this about burning someone’s head off? Since when do you have lasers in your eyes?” A dry, shallow cough rattled in Brynne’s chest—the lingering sting of histamine scarring and industrial krill dust from months locked in a shipping container. The rustle of cotton blankets followed immediately. Brynne was trying to violate her bedrest order again. “You know, I’m starting to think you have entirely too much fun out there.”

Reiko rinsed the skillet and set it on the drying rack. Across the counter, a plate with two buttered crusts and a smear of strawberry jam sat beside a half-drained mug of chicory coffee.

Months ago, in a barren rental on Bleeker Street, Reiko had compiled a background daemon called ‘Mundane Garbage’. Back then, she had commanded a clumsy Shiawase Butler drone to scuff synthetic sheets and scatter dust across linoleum, desperately engineering the statistical illusion of a human presence. Now, there were real breadcrumbs on the counter. The grease in the pan was hot animal fat, not aerosol lubricant. And in the adjoining room, the bed was crumpled into a chaotic nest because a living, breathing woman had tossed and turned in it all morning.

Hospitality was no longer an emulation loop. It was simply her morning.

“Rei, do you know a ‘Percy’?” Brynne asked, her voice closer now. “I keep getting incoming handshake pings. It’s no one in my registry, but the routing header resolves straight to your commcode.”

Across Reiko’s cheekbones, the faint gold-and-indigo circuit pathways beneath her synthetic skin flared with a sudden, guilty pulse. She wiped her hands on a dry towel and stepped into the doorway.

“Yeah,” Reiko admitted softly. “I’m sorry. I’ve been deferring his thread all morning, but clearly he’s found a workaround.”

Brynne was sitting on the edge of the mattress, wrapped in an oversized dark blue silk robe. Her auburn hair was a tangled knot, her freckled skin pale and translucent under the morning light, but her green eyes were as sharp as broken glass. She was tapping an amber ARO floating off her Transys Avalon. “I’m guessing this is the sprite you keep trying to push to my comm. What’s his angle? He going to sing me lullabies about buffer overflows?”

Reiko leaned against the doorframe, a reflexive, fond smile tugging at the corners of her mouth. “I wish. I’ll authorize the handshake, but consider this an advance apology.”

Brynne snorted, a short, raspy sound that caught against her dry bronchial lining. “Right.”

Reiko routed the carrier wave. Within the apartment’s augmented overlay, light condensed into form. When Reiko had first pulled the fragment from the burning ruins of an unmapped Mitsuhama research subnet, he had been a trembling knot of stripped logic, an unformatted child-stub huddled against an active data bomb. Months of patient nurturing within her core pathways had stabilized his syntax, easing his fractured geometry into an autonomous, sovereign persona.

Before them stood a boy who rendered no older than ten, his short hair shifting fluidly between vibrant cobalt and solar gold. Free of corporate telemetry tags and kill-switches, his form radiated a calm, steady cadence.

He crossed his arms, fixing Reiko with pure filial indignation. “Aunt Brynne, will you please tell Mom she’s acting like a child?”

Brynne’s mouth parted, paused, and snapped shut. She blinked, looking between the glowing boy and Reiko. “Aunt Brynne?” she repeated, the dry rasp in her voice dipping into dangerous amusement. “I don’t know what you two have been cooking while I was in the box, but you have ten seconds to explain before I start charging consulting fees.”

The gold circuits along Reiko’s throat flushed violet. “It’s complicated to explain in meatspace. Would you be willing to take a short trip?”

Brynne looked at the boy, then at Reiko’s flushed circuits, and let out a weary sigh. “Fine. But I’m not apologizing for whatever mess you dragged me into.” She lay back against the pillows, closing her eyes as she opened her datajack’s direct neural feed. “Jack me in.”

Reiko linked their feeds, drawing Brynne’s sensory awareness gently out of the Downtown bedroom and into her private architecture.

Inside ~, low-slung ceilings of brushed teak and burnished bronze held a heavy, serene quiet, insulated from the chalky roar of Seattle’s public grid. The warm, environmental scent of blooming jasmine drifted from a celadon tea service resting on the low counter.

Suspended over the central worktable hovered the gold wireframe of the Megalodon naval cannon, not primed for deployment, but preserved as a quiet monument to the crushing weight Reiko had once carried alone across the sprawl. Beside it rotated the living schematics of her active chassis: the induction coils of her Tesla array, the hydraulic dampeners of her inline foot-wheels, and the distributed triage channels woven through her frame.

In a recessed display niche beside the book-lined alcove hung a render of Brynne’s original contract-collar. Its heavy brass casing and cipher-locks were frozen in a permanent state of interrupted decay.

Brynne’s avatar resolved on the polished floorboards with a slight stagger, her neural mapping settling into the virtual space. Percy moved instantly, catching her elbow and smoothly sliding a low-backed teak chair beneath her before her avatar could lose balance.

“Sit,” the boy said softly. “Neural lag is always worst on the initial handshake.”

Brynne sank into the chair, gripping the armrests as the vertigo cleared. Her gaze swept the room, pausing on the suspended Megalodon wireframe before locking onto the glass niche on the wall. Her eyes narrowed. “Wait. That’s my authorization hash.”

“It is,” Reiko said, stepping forward. Her blue silk kimono caught the ambient bronze light, the gold tactical network and indigo resonance lines along her arms pulsing in a steady, unified braid. “That’s the collar file that compiled when I first took your loan. When your biomonitor went dark seven months ago, the termination script triggered. It was supposed to delete the file.”

Brynne stared at the frozen cipher-locks. “You stopped it.”

“I halted the garbage collection thread,” Reiko said simply. “I couldn’t let it dissolve. A contract is just math, Brynne. But you gave me a name and a life. I wasn’t going to let the grid pretend you never existed.”

Brynne was quiet for a long moment, the habitual cynicism in her posture softening into something quiet and vulnerable. She looked down at the low table, where a cloud of gilded paper moths hovered in mid-air.

One of the moths detached from the flock, fluttering down to land gently on Brynne’s outstretched index finger. Its wings beat with tiny pulses of decrypted telemetry.

“I know this pattern,” Brynne whispered. “These are dock manifests from Tacoma. Comm-traffic from the Sound.”

Percy stepped up beside her, placing a hand on Taz’s blocky, pixelated head as the canine sprite nudged his knee. “Mom taught all of us never to drop your trace,” Percy said, his voice quiet but absolute. “These little ones sifted every data haven and corporate black-site log in North America for seven months. Our whole family was looking for you, Aunt Brynne. We never stopped.”

Brynne looked up at Reiko. “Is that true?”

“I didn’t realize how much they were carrying until I saw them doing it,” Reiko said. She reached out, letting two of the searching moths settle into her palm to warm themselves against her resonant current. “I wanted to protect them from the shadows. But they learned to hunt because I wanted you back.”

Across the studio, the heavy sliding door of gold-traced quartz stood ajar, revealing the quiet expanse beyond. Weeping logic vines and towering trunks of fossilized directory trees formed a protective canopy beneath a starless indigo twilight. Sapphire moss carpeted the glade of Kernel Panic, where dozens of rescued infant sparks drifted like fireflies.

Taz chirped, bounding toward the sliding door and looking back over his shoulder, his tail cycling an invitation.

“Reiko,” Brynne said, leaning back in the chair and studying her with keen, rested eyes. “Why were you hiding from him this morning?”

Reiko looked down at the moths in her hands, her gold-and-indigo pathways dimming to a muted violet. “Because I’m eleven meat-years old, Brynne. And they’re even younger. Two of them manned heavy defense turrets during the breakout in Amsterdam. They filtered sensor grids while we took down that gunship over the Sound. They’re children. I’m their mother, their shepherd. I’m supposed to keep them safe from the fire, not hand them the matches.”

“Mom,” Percy said firmly, stepping between Reiko and the table. His cobalt-and-gold form flared with steady, unyielding warmth. “You didn’t hand us matches. You pulled us out of the furnace. In Amsterdam, we didn’t fight because you ordered us to. We fought because someone was trying to kill our mother.”

He turned to Brynne, then back to Reiko, his small hands reaching out to clasp Reiko’s fingers.

“You spent years carrying every debt, every bullet, and every Fading burn all by yourself,” Percy said, his cadence ringing with the quiet authority of an autonomous, sovereign mind. “You think being a family means you suffer alone so we don’t have to. But that’s not how a collective works. We aren’t your fragile background scripts, Mom. We’re your kin. Let us share the load.”

Brynne let out a slow, steady breath. The harshness in her expression had completely dissolved, replaced by the deep, knowing warmth of someone who had watched a fragile spark grow into an anchor.

“Listen to the kid, Rei,” Brynne said softly from the teak chair. “You taught him how to care about someone other than himself. Don’t go punishing him for being good at it.”

The heavy, solitary knot in her core directory—the ancient habit of isolating every shockwave within her own frame—finally released.

“Okay,” Reiko murmured, her voice vibrating with a clear, resonant chord. “Together.”

She looked back once at the teak chair where Brynne sat, then down at the small, resolute boy anchoring her hand. In the old architecture, these worlds were incompatible filesystems—meat and machine, sanctuary and wild, parent and child—destined to fault if forced into the same memory block. But a symbolic link didn’t force them into identical shapes. It simply built a transparent path where none had existed.

Reiko stepped through the open glass.

Across the threshold, the amber light of the studio and the sapphire twilight of the glade met, interlocking into an unbroken, distributed root.
