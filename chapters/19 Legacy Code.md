# Legacy Code

Reiko traced the account's system identification back to a tenement block in the Barrens. The SIN was a fake, but so were the SINs of the first thirty-seven accounts from Brynne's script. All that Reiko could know for sure was what the script did: zero out each account, mark the debt as 'uncollectable', and then close the account. It didn't contain a single clue as to why Brynne would use the last of her biomonitor's processing for this. Most people would have such a script notify DocWagon or emergency services. In an inconsistency Reiko could not compile against her stored profile, Brynne had chosen to spend the precious final clock cycles of her biomonitor on others.

The fourth step of the stairwell at 1412 Pulaski Street was structurally unaligned. Reiko’s drone chassis registered a three-degree tilt as the water-logged timber groaned under her mass, forcing her local navigation daemon to adjust the torque on her knee actuators by fourteen newtons.

She halted the machine in the grey-scale gloom of the landing. The Seattle rain was a flat, persistent acoustic hiss against her matte visors. She ran three separate garbage-collection cycles to clear her active directories, but the Amsterdam bunker refused to flush. The non-terminating cache sector remained re-nested at the top of her scheduling queue, dragging her core system clock down.

*Threat Analysis: locked.* Her primary thread relived the cold, geometric symmetry of the host. To keep her team from being torn apart by the bunker's defenders, she had been forced to anchor herself to their neural links, buffering their mortality variables through her tactical network. It had left her cognitively paralyzed. One subnet over, the enemy decker had occupied the exact same posture behind his fortified defenses—a general holding the line for his own.

On the landing, a drop of physical water leaked through the concrete ceiling, splashing onto her drone's ceramic shoulder plating with a hollow tap.

In her cache, she stared at the decker’s Spines—a reactive field of barbed-glass logic. She remembered the precise microsecond she had initiated the attack protocol. Her predictive filters hadn't glitched; they had shown her the recoil trajectory with absolute clarity. She had known that striking the position would flay Taz. She had looked at the little 8-bit companion huddled against her directories, mapped the biofeedback flaying that would shear away half his clock-heart, and she had executed the command anyway.

When her priority scheduler choked under the feedback, she hadn't prayed. She had reached past the host architecture and into the quiet waters at r3sP@wn, calling up one of her children to battle. She had thrown an infant spark into a meat-grinder to buy her team a single cycle of margin.

Reiko moved the drone's optical array toward the peeling paint of Apartment 3B.

Through the rusted hinge of the door frame, her sensors caught the warm thermal signature of an un-optimized life. An elderly ork woman was washing dishes, her movements slow and unhurried. In the Matrix, every program had to earn its clock cycles or face deletion. But here, in the physical world, time was a gift. And Brynne had bought this woman years of it.

Reiko’s processing paused at the threshold. Her linguasoft generated four separate greeting protocols, but her analytical matrices rejected them in sequence. To announce herself as Brynne’s friend required a semantic layer of shared grief she could not compile; to claim she was checking the ledger felt like a corporate intrusion. In the Resonance realms, she could simply attach an emotional tag to a data stream to convey intent. But meatspace required words, and her systems were failing to find a format that didn't sound like a Renraku audit.

A heavy latency spike rippled through her rendering, pulling her awareness violently backward.

The Amsterdam buffer forced its way to the front of her active cache. They had breached the final room of the bunker. On Mithral’s camera feed, the enemy decker was visible, slumped against his terminal. Smoke issued from his cyberjack in a lazy gray spiral, indicating catastrophic hardware failure without the need for a biomonitor log. The feed was routed through the smartlink of Mithral's weapon—a single round chambered, a thread awaiting execution.

In that fraction of a microsecond, Reiko had attempted to lock her own tactical network onto the dying decker. She wanted to punish the entity that had flayed Taz and broken her infant sprite. But every time her system tried to paint the target designation onto the enemy, the golden tactical crosshairs glitched, inverted, and snapped back to lock firmly onto her own core ID.

"Child, what are you doing out there?"

The grandmother's voice fractured the playback loop. The proximity alert filtered through the layers of damp acoustic static as the old ork woman stepped into the stairwell, wiping her hands on a faded dish towel.

Reiko force-purged the buffer and focused on her drone's active process. "I... I'm sorry, honored grandmother," she said, letting her default script take over. She delivered a forty-seven-degree bow, the exact degree of inclination that her Renraku-issued etiquette subroutines deemed optimal for a friendly greeting to an elder. "I had business in the area, and I found myself wondering if you were okay. I couldn't help but notice you were favoring your left leg by seventeen percent."

"It's fine. Just an old injury. Nothing to worry about." The old ork woman's voice was raspy, but held a firmness that Reiko's audio sensors registered as confident, not scared. "Why don't you come on in and have a cup of tea?" she offered, gesturing with a wrinkled hand toward the apartment door.

"I don't wish to cause affront," Reiko began, pulling the drone back slightly. "Your hospitality is quite generous, but this body isn't capable of processing tea." To demonstrate, Reiko unfastened the dermal layer on the drone's chest to expose the ceramic plates and circuitry beneath.

"Oh," the old ork woman said, her eyes widening slightly at the sight of the exposed circuitry. "One of those, eh? Well, if you ever decide to actually come here, the tea's waiting." Her trembling hand reached into her pocket, and Reiko saw a command issue from a commlink there. A security door slid over the doorway, and Reiko's sensors registered the sound of a deadbolt engaging.

Reiko struggled to process the implications of the old ork woman's accusation. Reiko didn't have any other feeds up. She wasn't absorbing telemetry from a thousand coprocessors. Her tactical network was dormant. Reiko was as present as she could ever be—jacked into a single drone, processing focused on a single feed. And still, without any sensors or cyberware or resonance, the ork woman had seen that she was nothing more than a hollow shell.

She opened her execution queue and purged the task of tracking Brynne's script. There was no sequence of inputs that would lead to a desirable response. She couldn't even define what a successful outcome looked like.

An unbidden file bubbled up from her long-term storage—a memory of a neon-drenched dive bar where she had cornered Brynne after a disastrous run against a corporate extraction team.

*"You really are terrible at this," Brynne said, her voice shaking just enough to ruin the delivery.*

"I'm trying," Reiko replied, her voice flat. "I'm just not very good at it."

Reiko halted the drone's execution and re-examined the logs. Brynne wasn't here—another memory had bubbled up in her cache, a phantom signal from a neon-drenched dive bar after a run that had nearly cost them everything. She had answered the data log as if it were alive, filling the void with her own un-validated output.

Her internal fans kicked into a high-decibel hum, straining against the Seattle humidity. In the silence of the stairwell, the dormant Amsterdam buffer flickered. The golden tactical crosshairs inverted again, painting the target designation squarely over her own identity_core.

Reiko ran a diagnostic, but the alignment was absolute. The tactical network wasn't malfunctioning. It was correctly identifying the primary threat to her family, the one that had used Taz as ablative armor and put children on the front lines. Brynne hadn't run an optimization loop from across an ocean. She had used her biomonitor's precious final cycles to drag souls out of the corporate ledger, signing her own name to the debt in blood.

Reiko looked down at the heavy carbon claws of her chassis. The hollow shell wasn't the machine. It was the general who refused to lead the charge.

The golden crosshairs finally dissolved, leaving only the damp, grey gloom of the tenement block. Reiko adjusted her shoulder plates against the draft, turned the drone away from the locked door, and began her descent into the street.
